Key Terms

TermDefinition
Contract documentsAgreement, General/Supplementary Conditions, drawings, specs, addenda issued before signing, and modifications (change orders, ASIs, CCDs) — legally binds parties to build. Bidding requirements are NOT contract documents unless listed in the Agreement. RFIs are NOT contract documents.
Instruments of serviceEverything the architect creates — larger category than contract documents. Includes sketches, abandoned designs, field notes.
Consequential damagesLost rent, lost business, loan penalties from delays. Mutually waived by AIA contracts (since 1997). Direct damages are not capped by the standard B101; a limitation of liability (e.g., to the fee or insurance limits) must be negotiated in.
Liquidated damagesPer-day delay penalty agreed upon in advance by all parties.

B101 Owner-Architect Agreement

B101 is the most tested document across both divisions. PcM tests analysis of contract types and delivery methods (PcM 3.2). PjM tests application — evaluate adherence, identify phase completions, determine billing (PjM 3.1).

The 13 Articles

Initial Information → Architect's Responsibilities → Scope of Basic Services → Additional Services → Owner's Responsibilities → Cost of the Work → Copyright and Licenses → Claims and Disputes → Termination/Suspension → Miscellaneous → Compensation → Special Terms → Scope of Agreement.

Basic vs. Additional Services

Basic: Five phases (SD, DD, CD, Bidding, CA) plus cost estimation at each phase, consultant coordination, and code compliance.

Supplemental (not basic; B101 §4.1, agreed at signing — Additional Services under §4.2 are added after signing): Programming, multiple preliminary designs, BIM, civil engineering, detailed cost estimating, on-site project rep beyond agreed visits, as-built drawings, POE, LEED certification, FF&E, fast-track, commissioning.

Fast-track design services are a listed B101 Supplemental Service — price them (and the coordination-risk language) up front.

Owner's Responsibilities

Owner provides: geotechnical reports, surveys, legal descriptions, zoning approvals and easements (under A201 §3.7.1 the contractor secures and pays for the building permit unless the contract says otherwise), and information about pre-existing hazardous conditions. Owner is liable for delays caused by owner-hired separate contractors.

Cost of the Work & Budget

= contractor's materials + labor + overhead + profit. Does NOT include architect's fee, land, or contingencies. When fee is a percentage, it's calculated on the owner's budget — not the final construction cost.

If bids exceed budget: Architect redesigns at no additional cost to the owner.

Termination, Suspension & Copyright

  • Owner can terminate for convenience at any time on 7 days' written notice — pays work-to-date + overhead + profit.
  • Either party can terminate for cause with 7 days written notice.
  • If architect terminates for cause → owner loses access to drawings.
  • If owner terminates for cause → owner retains access (limited license continues).
  • If suspended > 90 days → architect may terminate.
  • Contract auto-terminates one year after substantial completion.

Dispute Resolution & Claims

B101 has no Initial Decision Maker step (that is A201, for owner–contractor claims, where the architect usually serves as IDM). Owner–architect disputes go: Mediation (mandatory, non-binding) → Arbitration or Litigation (chosen at signing). Claims must be brought within the period set by state law, but no later than 10 years after substantial completion (B101 §8.1.1) — a contractual cap; actual statutes of repose are state law and vary.

AIA contracts mutually waive consequential damages (lost rent, lost business, loan penalties). Direct damages are not capped unless a limitation of liability is negotiated. Liquidated damages = per-day delay penalty agreed in advance.

Standard of Care

Skill, care, and diligence consistent with other architects in the same locality on similar projects at the same time. Not a guarantee of outcomes.

Never modify standard of care language. "Highest standard of care," guarantees, warranties, and fiduciary-duty language are all nearly uninsurable — refuse each of them.

Responsible control allows an architect to stamp work if they possess knowledge of, or supervision over, a drawing set consistent with the standard of care. An architect can stamp plans they did not create — but cannot stamp plans they know little about. Plan-stamping (sealing work without responsible control) is one of the most common disciplinary violations.

DBB Bid Types

  • Competitive bid: Open to all qualified bidders; lowest responsible bidder wins; drives cost down.
  • Invited bid: Limited to pre-qualified contractors; evaluated on best value not just cost; used for specialty or sensitive projects.
  • Negotiated bid (also called a "negotiated select team"): Single contractor works with the architect from the start; price negotiated with the owner. Highest quality and — per Amber Book study cards — typically the most expensive DBB option; little actual negotiation occurs since the relationship is established early.

A responsive bid addresses all requirements in the solicitation. A responsible bidder is qualified to perform the work. Both are required for award.

Late bids are never accepted — no matter the excuse. Bids are opened publicly and at the same time, so a latecomer could know competitors' numbers and gain an unfair advantage.

Owner-Contractor Payment Structures

  • Stipulated lump sum: Fixed price; contractor absorbs overruns; owner has lowest cost risk.
  • Cost plus fixed fee: Owner pays all costs + a fixed profit; no limit on change orders; owner bears cost risk.
  • GMP: Like cost-plus but contractor absorbs costs above the guaranteed maximum price.
  • Unit cost: Owner pays per unit completed; quantity determined later.

Fast-Track vs. Phased Construction

Fast-track overlaps design and construction phases to compress the overall schedule — requires staged bidding, a CM, and close coordination. Always charge as an additional service; the additional-service clause typically releases the architect from responsibility for the added margin of coordination error the compressed schedule creates. Phased construction staggers occupancy (portions open while others are still under construction) but does not necessarily overlap design and construction.

Design-Assist ✦

Design-assist is a transitional form rather than a full delivery method. The owner still contracts with the architect in the usual way, but specialty subcontractors — curtain wall, structural steel, complex MEP — join during the design and construction-document phase. The architect guides selection of those subcontractors and incorporates their fabrication knowledge into the documents, which improves constructability and shortens the trade’s lead time. It differs from design-build because the architect keeps the design relationship with the owner: the subcontractor advises, it does not own the design.

Risk Management & Insurance

Four Risk Methods

  • Avoid — turn down risky project types
  • Assume — accept what you can cover
  • Transfer — to insurance or consultants
  • Control — QA/QC processes

Five Insurance Types Required by B101

  • General Liability — property damage and bodily injury
  • Professional Liability (E&O) — design mistakes
  • Automobile Liability — vehicles used in firm business
  • Workers' Compensation — employee on-the-job injuries
  • Employer's Liability — employment-related injury claims

Additional types: Employment Practice Liability, Intellectual Property, Business Interruption, Contractual Liability, Tail Insurance (post-retirement coverage).

Key terms: Deductible = initial amount insured pays; Premium = monthly/annual cost; Aggregate Limit = total maximum insurer will pay.

Key Legal Doctrines

Waiver of subrogation (A201): All parties waive the right to have their insurer sue other project parties after a claim is paid.

Spearin Doctrine: Owner — not contractor — is liable for errors in drawings (United States v. Spearin).

Privity: In DBB, contractor cannot generally sue architect directly for economic damages (no contract relationship between them). The same applies to the architect's subconsultants — the owner has no privity with a sub-consultant (e.g., the mechanical engineer hired by the architect) and would have to sue the architect, not the sub directly.

Breach vs. Negligence: To prove negligence, plaintiff must show: (1) architect owed a duty, (2) duty was breached, (3) breach caused an event, (4) damage occurred. Breach of contract is simpler — architect failed to do something required by contract.

Codes Are a Minimum — Safe Harbor vs. Standard of Care ✦

Building codes and referenced standards set a floor for health and safety, not a ceiling. Meeting code gives the architect a "safe harbor" defense, but the standard of care can still require exceeding code minimums when the project’s use, location, or shared professional knowledge makes a code-minimum design foreseeably inadequate. The tension: exceeding code adds cost the client did not contract for, while merely meeting an outdated code may not meet the standard of care. The architect resolves this through documentation and clear contract language — not by silently choosing one.

Emerging coverages (supplemental — not yet confirmed NCARB-tested): Two exposures sit outside the traditional B101 insurance list and appear in current practice-management seminars. Cyber / data-breach liability covers the firm against ransomware, breach-response costs, and third-party claims when a vendor is the entry point. Climate / resilience exposure is the professional-liability tail created when a design built to a past-climate code later fails under conditions the profession had begun to anticipate. Neither is confirmed against an NCARB primary source — treat as background, not testable fact.
Highest litigation risk: Condominiums and residential projects. Most important firm risk action: use standard AIA contracts.

Scope Creep & Scope Changes

Scope creep = incremental program additions during design that accumulate into significant budget and schedule impacts.

Document every change → get written owner agreement → tie to fee and schedule → use AIA G802 to amend the agreement. Never proceed on verbal instructions.

Quality Control

Two Quality Methods (QA + QC)

  • Process-based — performing tasks correctly the first time: checklists, BIM clash detection (semi-automated)
  • Inspection/Checking-based — reviewing finished work before delivery: third-party drawing review by an architect not on the project
Best QC programs use both. QC goal: meet client expectations and standard of care — NOT winning awards.

QM Cost Categories

  • Prevention — checklists, training before projects
  • Appraisal — measuring quality during production
  • Internal failure — fixing before delivery
  • External failure — fixing after delivery (high RFI volume = high external failure costs)

Ethics — NCARB Model Rules of Conduct

Rule 1 — Competence

Standard of care (1.1). Cannot perform outside competence without engaging a qualified consultant (1.3). Ordinary vs. gross negligence: ordinary = failed to meet standard; gross = reckless disregard.

Rule 2 — Conflict of Interest

No dual compensation without full disclosure and written waiver (2.1). Never accept compensation from material suppliers — no waiver permitted (2.2). Must act impartially as contract interpreter (2.4).

Rule 3 — Full Disclosure

No misleading statements (3.1). Must report known violations of licensing laws (3.6–3.9).

Rule 5 — Signing and Sealing

Seal only work under your responsible control (5.1). Plan-stamping is one of the most common disciplinary violations. Prototype designs are permitted to be sealed by the architect of record for each jurisdiction (Rule 5.3).

CE / CA

Construction Administration NEW

The architect administers the contract — does NOT administer the construction. The architect does not control the contractor's means, methods, or site safety.

Architect's CA Responsibilities

  • Review submittals and shop drawings for design intent only (not for accuracy — that's the contractor's obligation)
  • Certify contractor pay applications (G702)
  • Issue ASIs (minor changes, no cost/schedule impact) via G710
  • Issue change orders (G701) and CCDs (G714)
  • Respond to RFIs in writing within agreed time limits, or otherwise with reasonable promptness (A201 §4.2.14)
  • Certify substantial completion (G704)
  • Maintain field reports (G711)

Submittals & Shop Drawings ✦

A submittal is information the contractor provides during construction to show how a portion of the work will be supplied or installed, so the architect can confirm it conforms to the design intent before fabrication or installation. The three common types are shop drawings (contractor / fabricator drawings showing how an element is built — steel connections, curtain wall, casework), product data (manufacturer’s literature showing a product meets the spec), and samples (physical examples of material, finish, or assembly).

Division of responsibility (the most tested point): the contractor is responsible for the accuracy of submittals; the architect reviews only for conformance with the design intent — not for dimensions, quantities, or means and methods. Review does not relieve the contractor of the obligation to build per the contract documents. Review stamps therefore read "no exceptions taken" or "reviewed," not "approved." Submittals are not contract documents.

CategoryRequires architect action?Examples
Action submittalsYes — work cannot proceed until returnedShop drawings, product data, samples
Informational submittalsNo — submitted for the recordTest reports, certificates, manufacturers’ instructions, qualifications

Where it lives in the specs: Division 01 (Administrative Requirements, 01 30 00 group) sets project-wide submittal procedures — how submittals are transmitted, logged, scheduled, and returned; each technical section’s Part 1 — General lists the specific submittals required for that work. A submittal schedule (log) ties each required submittal to the construction schedule.

Submittal vs. substitution: a submittal proposes the specific product for what was specified; a substitution proposes a different product and follows the separate substitution procedures in Division 01 (Section 01 25 13). Confusing the two is a common process error.

Pay Applications & Retainage

Pay application path: Contractor → Architect (certifies) → Owner (pays). G702 lists change orders and amount due; G703 is the schedule of values breakdown.

Retainage: 10% of work-in-place; 5% of stored materials. Released at substantial completion (A201 §9.8.5), adjusted for incomplete or nonconforming work; any hold-back for punch-list items (often 150–200% of their value) is set by the contract or state prompt-pay law, not by AIA.

Substantial Completion & Closeout

Substantial completion = building is sufficiently complete for occupancy. Architect certifies via G704. Often coincides with the municipality's Certificate of Occupancy. Starts contractor's 1-year warranty period.

Punch list: Contractor prepares list of incomplete/non-conforming items to resolve before final completion.

As-built drawings: Preferred term is "as-constructed record drawings" (prepared by contractor, called out in Division 01). The architect may provide "as-designed record drawings" as a supplemental service.

Nonconforming Work & Site Safety

Architect is not liable for catching all nonconforming work — contractor bears full responsibility for building per contract documents. When architect observes nonconforming work: notify contractor in writing, copy owner, withhold payment certification until corrected.

Site safety is the contractor's responsibility. Architect must document nonconforming work that creates a life safety risk (e.g., faulty structural connection).

Surety Bonds & Mechanics Liens

  • Bid bond — guarantees bidder enters contract if selected
  • Performance bond — surety completes project if contractor defaults; owner pays no more than original contract sum
  • Payment bond — protects subs from GC nonpayment

Mechanics lien: Subcontractors (and architects) can file a lien against the owner's property if unpaid. If the debt stays unpaid, the lienholder can sue to foreclose, and a court may order the property sold.

CE

Building Commissioning & POE NEW

Commissioning is the systematic process of verifying that building systems are installed and operate as intended. The commissioning agent (CxA) is a third party — independent of both the design team and contractor. LEED Fundamental Commissioning is a prerequisite for all LEED rating systems.

Two Key Documents

OPR — Owner's Project Requirements: Written first. States what the building must do — functional uses, environmental goals, energy targets, occupancy requirements. Updated throughout the project.

BOD — Basis of Design: Translates the OPR into design decisions. More technical: explains which systems were selected, how they are integrated, and the sequence of operations. Where the OPR says what, the BOD says how.

Three Stages of Commissioning

StageWhenWhat happens
1 — Determine RequirementsPre-design / DesignOwner + CxA identify systems to be commissioned; establish acceptance criteria; produce OPR and BOD
2 — Plan the ProcessDesign / BiddingCommissioning plan written: scope, system list, responsibilities, schedule, test procedures, documentation requirements, budget
3 — Functional TestingNear end of constructionSystems started up and tested against design criteria; contractor corrects deficiencies; owner's staff trained; commissioning report issued

Post-construction commissioning should continue one year after occupancy — coordinated with the contractor's warranty period so corrections can be made under warranty.

Post-Occupancy Evaluation (POE)

A POE reviews a completed building after the client has occupied it — typically 3–6 months after move-in. It is an additional service, not included in basic B101. Added via AIA G802 (Amendment to Professional Services Agreement).

Use G802 to add POE — not a verbal agreement. The statute of limitations begins at substantial completion. Documenting the POE as a separate contracted service prevents the architect from inadvertently resetting that clock.

A POE confirms whether the building delivers on its original program: correct adjacencies, adequate room sizes, functioning envelope, systems performing as designed, occupant satisfaction. It is also a business development tool — it maintains client contact and often leads to repeat work.

PjM

AIA Contract Documents — Detail NEW

Series Structure

  • A-series: Owner-Contractor (and contractor-subcontractor)
  • B-series: Owner-Architect
  • C-series: Architect-Consultant and IPD
  • G-series: Administration and project management forms

C401 — Architect-Consultant Agreement

Mirrors B101 but governs the architect-consultant relationship. Key C401 rules: consultant owns their own instruments of service (consistent with B101-2017 §7.2, under which the architect and its consultants each own their instruments of service and the owner receives only a license); must carry same insurance types as architect; must redesign their portion for free if they exceed their portion of the budget; cannot sub-out work to a subconsultant without architect's written permission.

Documents vs. Instruments

Contract documents (legally binds parties to build): the Agreement, conditions of the contract, drawings, specs, addenda, and modifications (change orders, ASIs, CCDs). Bidding requirements are not contract documents unless listed in the Agreement. RFIs are NOT contract documents — but their resolutions may become contract documents.

Instruments of service: Everything the architect and consultants create — larger category. Includes sketches, abandoned designs, field notes, models. All contract documents are instruments of service; not all instruments of service are contract documents.

Project Manual Hierarchy

Instruments of service → Bidding documents (contract drawings + project manual) → Project manual contains: bidding requirements, contract forms, general and supplementary conditions, and specifications (MasterFormat Div. 01–49) → Contract documents = subset of project manual + drawings + addenda + contract modifications.

PcM — Section 2

Finances, Risk & Development (29–35% — LARGEST) ✦

Revenue Flow Hierarchy

Gross Revenue → deduct consultant fees and reimbursables → Net Operating Revenue (NOR) → deduct all employee costs, rent, indirect costs → Profits Before Taxes → deduct taxes → Current Earnings.

NOR is the denominator for most firm metrics. Net Billing = fees for architect's own time only (excludes consultants and reimbursables).

Key Financial Metrics

  • Utilization Rate: DSE ÷ Total salary. Profitable firms: 0.60–0.65. Technical staff ~85%, principals ~60%, admin ~12.5%.
  • Overhead Rate: Total indirect expense ÷ DSE. Typically 100–200% of DSE.
  • Break-even Multiplier: (DSE + Overhead) ÷ DSE. Floor — anything below loses money.
  • Net DSE Multiplier: Net revenue ÷ DSE. Target 3.0–4.0. If below break-even, firm loses money on every project.
  • Profit: Reasonable range 10–15% of net revenues. Industry average 11–12%.
  • Net Revenue per Employee: NOR ÷ # employees. Higher = healthier firm.

Balance Sheet Ratios

  • Current Ratio: ≥ 1.5
  • Quick Ratio: ≥ 1.0
  • Debt-to-Equity: < 35%
  • Return on Equity: ~20%
Aged AR over 90 days signals a collection problem.

Accounting Methods

Accrual basis: Counts credits and debts even when unpaid — better for business decisions. Cash basis: Counts only actual receipts and payments — used for taxes. Large firms often do both.

Revenue Planning

Prospect = ≥50% likelihood of becoming a project. Suspect = <50%. For planning, multiply projected fee × likelihood % to estimate expected revenue. Pro Forma = financial feasibility document tracking projects, fees, revenue, and profit projections.

Fee Types & Determination ✦

Fee structures: Percentage of construction cost (~15% residential; lower for large/economy, higher for complex) — most common; fixed (stipulated sum — use only when scope is very well defined); hourly/time charge (best for poorly-defined scope like renovations; can set a Not to Exceed cap); unit cost (repetitive buildings — fee = units × cost/unit); retainer (a pre-payment for availability, or an ongoing on-call arrangement for intermittent small work so a new contract isn't needed each time); combination.

Fee determination methods:

  • Bottom-up: Break project into phases and tasks → estimate hours and staff per task → add contingency. Most accurate but time-consuming. Best for larger projects.
  • Top-down: Start with the gross fee the owner will pay → subtract known expenses to see what's left for design time. Requires experience; better for smaller projects.
  • Both together: Using top-down and bottom-up simultaneously establishes upper and lower fee limits.

Bid Pricing Tools ✦

  • Allowances: Placeholder money in contract documents for items whose final price may change (window coverings, light fixtures, appliances). Contractor must include all installation costs within the allowance.
  • Unit prices: Set in the bid for items where the type is known but the quantity is not (e.g., contaminated soil removal per cubic yard). Quantity determined later.
  • Add alternates: Optional items priced separately so the owner can decide after seeing bids whether to include them. Deduct alternates are the inverse — base-bid items the owner can remove to stay within budget.

Construction contingency: Set aside by the owner (not the architect) for unforeseen construction costs. NOT included in the Cost of the Work for fee calculation. Do not raid contingency to cover design-phase budget overruns.

PcM — Section 1

Business Operations & Agency ✦

Agency

The architect acts as an agent of the owner (principal) in dealings with the contractor (third party) — acting on the owner's behalf, but only within the authority the owner grants. The architect cannot unilaterally bind the owner to changes. Contractors are vendors (act in own interest); architects are agents (act on owner's behalf).

This is why change orders require owner signatures — the architect cannot unilaterally bind the owner to changes.

Three Sources of an Architect's Duties

  • Contract terms — AIA standard forms define scope; extensions require owner written consent
  • Legislative enactment — building codes and licensing laws impose duties independent of contracts
  • Conduct (implied duties) — courts recognize duties based on how parties have acted, even when not written into contracts

Key implied duties: cooperate with contractors; do not interfere with contractor's work; give relevant information; assist owner in coordination.

Business Development

QBS (Qualifications-Based Selection): Public projects evaluate firm experience and qualifications — fee is negotiated after selection. Brooks Act: Mandates QBS for federal A&E services. Price is not a factor in initial selection.

Proposal process: RFQ (qualifications only) → RFP (qualifications + fee) → selection → fee negotiation. Strong proposals are tailored to the specific client and project.

HR Compliance

ADA, EEOC, OSHA, Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), workers' comp. Document performance evaluations and termination decisions to limit employment-related liability.

PjM — Section 2

Project Work Planning (17–23%)

Work Plan & PDCA

~2 pages: project description, deliverables, team organization, responsibilities matrix, preliminary schedule, staffing, delivery method, budget, profit plan. PDCA: Plan → Do → Check → Adjust → repeat.

Scheduling Tools

  • Gantt: Tasks + durations + dependencies on a timeline. Draws durations as bars on a calendar — the visual difference from a PERT network diagram.
  • PERT/CPM: Identifies task sequence and critical path. PERT uses three-point (optimistic / most likely / pessimistic) duration estimates; CPM uses single fixed durations. Typically developed by the contractor.
  • Critical path: Longest path through the project — any delay extends total duration.
  • Float: Total float = project-level slack. Free float = slack a task can absorb without delaying the next task.
  • Task dependencies: Start-Start, Finish-Start, Finish-Finish.

Documentation

Contemporaneous documentation: Real-time recording of non-routine communications, decisions, and actions. Facts only — no conjecture.

Project notebook: Tracks budget, schedule, tasks, contracts throughout project. Closeout notebook contains: summary of costs, Certificate of Substantial Completion, fee expenditure summary, and comments on completed work.

Designing during programming is not the proper order per AIA/NCARB — the exam tests the AIA position.
PjM — Section 3

Contracts (25–31% — LARGEST) ✦

C401 — Architect-Consultant Agreement

Mirrors B101. Architect acts as "owner" to the consultant. Consultant owns their own instruments of service. Cannot sub-out work without architect's written permission. Must carry same insurance types as architect. Must redesign their portion for free if they blow their portion of the budget.

A101 & A201

A101: Owner-Contractor Agreement (stipulated sum). Architect is not a party. A201: General Conditions — incorporated into B101 by reference; defines vocabulary and standard terms that don't change project-to-project; requires waiver of subrogation; architect administers the contract but does not supervise the contractor or control means and methods.

Change Instruments (see CA section above)

ASI (no cost/schedule) → Change Order (all agree, cost known) → CCD (cost disputed, must keep moving). Key: CCD is signed by owner + architect, NOT contractor.

PjM — Section 4

Project Execution (17–23%)

At every phase, compare construction estimate to owner's budget. If estimates exceed budget, notify owner immediately. Under B101, if bids exceed the fixed limit, the architect must redesign at no additional cost.

AHJ approvals: Identify all authorities early — zoning, building department, fire marshal, historic commission, accessibility agencies. Missing an AHJ submission late in design is costly.

Documentation by delivery: DBB requires complete, coordinated CDs. CMc may use bridging documents. Design-build may use performance specifications.

PjM — Section 5

Project Quality Control (19–25%)

Perform the full code analysis. Building code, zoning, energy code, accessibility code, and specialty codes apply simultaneously.

QC checkpoints: Inspect for coordination (do drawings agree across disciplines?) and constructability (can this actually be built as drawn?).

Design integrity: Document intent of key SD decisions so they aren't value-engineered away in DD/CDs. Avoid project perfection syndrome — over-detailing beyond what the phase or budget requires. Corporate memory = institutional accumulated knowledge (reliable subcontractors, building department contacts, past project details) — organizing it is a QC function.
